\section{Neural Networks and the Matrix Multiplication}
\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Basic Neural Net (MLP/FFN)}
  \centering
  \only<1>{
    \resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{../assets/figures/nn_0}}
  }
  \only<2>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{../assets/figures/nn_1}}
    \[y_1 = x_1 w_{11} + x_2 w_{12} + x_3 w_{13} + x_4 w_{14}\]
  }
  \only<3>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{../assets/figures/nn_2}}
    \[y_2 = x_1 w_{21} + x_2 w_{22} + x_3 w_{23} + x_4 w_{24}\]
  }
  \only<4>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{../assets/figures/nn_3}}
    \[y_3 = x_1 w_{31} + x_2 w_{32} + x_3 w_{33} + x_4 w_{34}\]
  }
  \only<5>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{../assets/figures/nn_4}}
    \[y_4 = x_1 w_{41} + x_2 w_{42} + x_3 w_{43} + x_4 w_{44}\]
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Linear Algebra Interpretation}
  Here, notice that for each \(y_i\), we are taking a weighted sum of the
  \(x\) elements. That is,

  \[y_i = x_1 w_{i1} + x_2 w_{i2} + x_3 w_{i3} + x_4w_{i4}.\]

  Arranging the \(y_i\) into a vector, we get

  \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
      y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \\ y_4
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
      w_{11} & w_{12} & w_{13} & w_{14} \\
      w_{21} & w_{22} & w_{23} & w_{24} \\
      w_{31} & w_{32} & w_{33} & w_{34} \\
      w_{41} & w_{42} & w_{43} & w_{44}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
      x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4
    \end{bmatrix}.
  \]

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Linear Algebra Interpretation}
  We can generalize the number of \(x\)'s and the number of \(y\)'s.

  \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
      y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_m
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
      w_{11} & w_{12} & \cdots & w_{1n} \\
      w_{21} & w_{22} & \cdots & w_{2n} \\
      \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
      w_{m1} & w_{m2} & \cdots & w_{mn}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
      x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n
    \end{bmatrix}.
  \]

  We say, \(n\) is the \emph{input dimension} and \(m\) is the \emph{output dimension}.
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Computing with multiple \(x\) vectors.}
  Suppose we have multiple \(x\) vectors, for which we want to pass through our
  neural network. We can stack the multiple \(x\) vectors in the \textbf{batch},
  which we compute with a matrix multiplication.

  \[
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
      \begin{bmatrix}
        y_1^{(1)} & y_1^{(2)} & \cdots & y_1^{(B)} \\
        y_2^{(1)} & y_2^{(2)} & \cdots & y_2^{(B)} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        y_m^{(1)} & y_m^{(2)} & \cdots & y_m^{(B)}
      \end{bmatrix}
      =
      \begin{bmatrix}
        w_{11} & w_{12} & \cdots & w_{1n} \\
        w_{21} & w_{22} & \cdots & w_{2n} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        w_{m1} & w_{m2} & \cdots & w_{mn}
      \end{bmatrix}
      \begin{bmatrix}
        x_1^{(1)} & x_1^{(2)} & \cdots & x_1^{(B)} \\
        x_2^{(1)} & x_2^{(2)} & \cdots & x_2^{(B)} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        x_n^{(1)} & x_n^{(2)} & \cdots & x_n^{(B)}
      \end{bmatrix}.
    $}
  \]

  We write this succinctly as \(Y = WX\), where \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times B}\),
  \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times B}\), and \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\).

  \begin{alertblock}{Yippee!}
    We have successfully mapped the batched computation of a \textbf{linear layer}
    a matrix multiplication!
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Composing Layers}

  We can view \(W\) as a function that takes in an \(n\)-dimensional vector and
  maps it to an \(m\)-dimensional vector. \(W \colon \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m\).
  We create deep neural networks by \emph{composing} these transformations, along
  with  \textbf{nonlinearities} \(\sigma_i\).

  \input{../assets/figures/layer_composition}

  \begin{block}{Remark}
    Notice that this figure doesn't show the \emph{batch} dimension. However,
    if you work through the dimension mappings, the \emph{batch} dimension \(B\)
    will be unaffected by this composition.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Limitations for Neural Network Architecture}

  \input{../assets/figures/layer_composition}

  Architectures such as \textbf{Transformers} and \textbf{CNNs} have connectivity
  patterns more complex than a simple composition of layers. Nevertheless, linear
  transformations remain a fundamental computational primitive in neural network
  architectures.

  \begin{block}{A fundamental building block}
    A linear transformation may seem like a narrow operation to study, but modern
    neural networks perform it repeatedly and at enormous scale. Its efficiency
    therefore has an outsized effect on training and inference performance. The
    rest of this presentation explores the hardware and techniques that make these
    operations efficient.
  \end{block}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Takeaways}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A linear layer applied to a batch of inputs can be expressed as a single
      matrix multiplication, \(Y = WX\).
    \item Deep networks compose these linear transformations with nonlinearities.
      More complex architectures change how layers are connected, but linear
      transformations remain a fundamental building block.
    \item \textbf{Matrix multiplication therefore captures a large and reusable part of
      neural network computation.}
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{alertblock}{Why is this representation useful?}
    It separates what the network computes from how the hardware executes it.
    Instead of optimizing every architecture from scratch, we can focus on one
    common computational primitive. Next, we will examine how GPUs execute matrix
    multiplications efficiently.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Why GPUs? What's wrong with CPUs?}
\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Computing a Matrix Multiplication}
  Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two \(4\times4\) matrices. The matrix multiple \(C = AB\)
  results in another \(4\times4\) matrix.

  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{matmul.png}

  The operations are very simple, but the number of operations grow on the order
  of \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\), where \(n\) is the dimensions of the matrices.

  \begin{block}{Remark}
    Linear algebra and matrix multiplications show up everywhere in scientific
    computing, so computing them efficiently has always been in high demand.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]
  \frametitle{Runtime of a matrix multiplication on a CPU}
  \begin{onlyenv}<1>
    \begin{lstlisting}[
      basicstyle=\ttfamily\small,
      columns=fullflexible,
      breaklines=false,
      breakatwhitespace=false
    ]
for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
  for (std::size_t j = 0; j < n; ++j) {
    double sum = 0.0;
    for (std::size_t k = 0; k < n; ++k) {
      sum += a[i*n+k] * b[k*n+j];
    }
    c[i*n+j] = sum;
  }
}
    \end{lstlisting}
  \end{onlyenv}

  \begin{onlyenv}<2>
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{matmul_timing.png}
  \end{onlyenv}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Parallelism}
  GPU's came about due to the increase in computational power needed to render
  3D games in real time. This requires \emph{millions} of FLOP's per second.
  CPU's could not keep up.
  Rendering graphics has a nice property that we can take advantage of:
  \textbf{parallelism}.

  Each pixel on the screen doesn't really care any of the other pixels. Furthermore,
  the computation that is required for each pixel isn't that complex either.
  The difficulty in rendering graphics is the volume of computations.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Matrix Multiplication is Parallel}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{matmul.png}

  Each element of the output is independent. The results of \(C_{11}\) does not
  effect the results of \(C_{12}\), or any other element.

  \begin{block}{Remark}
    If matrix multiplication is a \textbf{parallel} task, and GPU's were designed for the
    \textbf{parallel} task of rendering graphics, can we repurpose GPU's to do matrix multiplication?
    \pause (Yes.)
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{What is GEMM?}
  \begin{block}{Definition: Single-precision general matrix multiplication (SGEMM)}
    \[
      C \leftarrow \alpha AB + \beta C
    \]
  \end{block}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Setting $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=0$ gives ordinary matrix multiplication: $C=AB$.
    \item Other values let one call scale the product and accumulate it into an existing matrix.
  \end{itemize}

  Our goal is unchanged math with less data movement and more useful work per
  thread, and sometimes we need to accumulate matrix multiplications.
  \begin{example}
    During the forward pass when training a model, we accumulate the gradients
    from the entire batch.
  \end{example}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Takeaway}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item CPUs can't efficiently perform matrix multiplication at scale.
    \item Graphics rendering is a parallel task, and so is matrix multiplication. Thus, GPUs can be good at matrix multiplication.
    \item GEMM is a generalization of of matrix multiplication, and is the operation we will be discussing.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Whats next}
    We have established that GPUs \emph{can} do matrix multiplication, but how
    do we actually get there?
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Optimizing \textbf{Ge}neral \textbf{M}atrix \textbf{M}ultiplication (GEMM) on CUDA}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{CUDA Basics}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The CPU is known as the \textbf{host}, and manages top level logic.
    \item The GPU is known as the \textbf{device}, and does the heavy lifting.
    \item The host launches \textbf{kernels} to the device.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item A kernel runs in parallel on my \textbf{threads} on the device.
    \item The number of threads is configured by the host, when it \textbf{launches} the kernel.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Threads are grouped into \textbf{blocks}.
    \item The collection of blocks requested by the host is organized in a \textbf{grid}.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]
  \frametitle{Naive SGEMM kernel}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.75\textwidth}
      \begin{lstlisting}[
        basicstyle=\ttfamily\fontsize{6}{6.8}\selectfont,
        escapeinside={(*@}{@*)}
      ]
__global__ void sgemm_naive(int M, int N, int K, float alpha, const float *A,
                            const float *B, float beta, float *C) {(*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (device-kernel);@*)
  const uint x = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x; (*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (thread-id);@*)
  const uint y = blockIdx.y * blockDim.y + threadIdx.y;

  if (x < M && y < N) {
    float tmp = 0.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < K; ++i)
      tmp += A[x*K+i] * B[i*N+y];
    C[x*N+y] = alpha*tmp + beta*C[x*N+y];
  }
}

void run_sgemm_naive(int M, int N, int K, float alpha, float *A, float *B,
                     float beta, float *C) {(*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (host-code);@*)
  dim3 blockDim(32, 32);
  dim3 gridDim(CEIL_DIV(M, 32), CEIL_DIV(N, 32));
  sgemm_naive<<<gridDim, blockDim>>>( (*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (kernel-launch);@*)
      M, N, K, alpha, A, B, beta, C);
}

int main() {
  // ... set up matrices and dimensions ...
  run_sgemm_naive(M, N, K, alpha, A, B, beta, C);
}
      \end{lstlisting}
    \end{column}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{We can do better}
  These benchmarks were run on an RTX 3090, whose key specifications are:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 10,496 CUDA cores with up to $35.6$ TFLOP/s of FP32 throughput.
    \item 24 GB of GDDR6X memory with $936$ GB/s of bandwidth.
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{block}{Theoretical lower bound on latency}
    For SGEMM with \(n=8192\):
    \[
      \begin{aligned}
        \text{work} &\approx 2n^3
        = 1.10\times10^{12}\ \text{FLOP}, \\
        t_{\min} &\approx
        \frac{1.10\ \text{TFLOP}}{35.6\ \text{TFLOP/s}}
        \approx 0.031\ \text{s} = 31\ \text{ms}.
      \end{aligned}
    \]
    \centering
    \textbf{Observed latency: \(3.55\) s---about \(115\times\) the theoretical minimum.}
  \end{block}
  \textbf{Accessing Device Memory is very slow.} 96.7\% of warp stalls are memory-related.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{What can we take advantage of?}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Blocks are scheduled to SMs; threads are scheduled on CUDA cores within SMs.
    \item Threads can save time by loading data from Device/Global memory into Shared Memory within the SM.
  \end{enumerate}

  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{cuda-programming-model.png}
  % from https://modal.com/gpu-glossary/device-software/cuda-programming-model
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Tiling}
  \begin{block}{Main Idea}
    Before doing any computation, load a chunk of the necessary data from DRAM
    to SMEM as a block. Then, memory accesses for the computation now only go to SMEM.
  \end{block}

  \centering
  \input{../assets/figures/basic_gpu_model.tex}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Basic tiled SGEMM: reuse data on chip}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A block cooperatively loads a tile of $A$ and a tile of $B$ from global memory into shared memory.
    \item Synchronize so every thread sees the complete tiles.
    \item Each thread accumulates a partial dot product using the cached values.
    \item Synchronize, advance along $K$, and repeat until the output tile is complete.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]
  \frametitle{Basic tiled SGEMM}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.75\textwidth}
      \begin{lstlisting}[
        basicstyle=\ttfamily\fontsize{7}{7.8}\selectfont,
        escapeinside={(*@}{@*)}
      ]
__shared__ float As[BLOCKSIZE * BLOCKSIZE];
__shared__ float Bs[BLOCKSIZE * BLOCKSIZE];
float tmp = 0.0;

for (int bkIdx = 0; bkIdx < K; bkIdx += BLOCKSIZE) {
  As[threadRow * BLOCKSIZE + threadCol] =
      A[threadRow * K + threadCol];
  Bs[threadRow * BLOCKSIZE + threadCol] =
      B[threadRow * N + threadCol]; (*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (tile-load);@*)

  __syncthreads(); (*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (tile-ready);@*)
  A += BLOCKSIZE;
  B += BLOCKSIZE * N;

  for (int dotIdx = 0; dotIdx < BLOCKSIZE; ++dotIdx) {
    tmp += As[threadRow * BLOCKSIZE + dotIdx] *
           Bs[dotIdx * BLOCKSIZE + threadCol]; (*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (tile-compute);@*)
  }

  __syncthreads(); (*@\tikz[remember picture,overlay]\coordinate (tile-next);@*)
}
      \end{lstlisting}
    \end{column}

    \begin{column}{0.25\textwidth}
      \begin{tikzpicture}[remember picture,
          callout/.style={draw,rounded corners,fill=#1!10,
          text width=0.92\linewidth,inner sep=3pt,font=\scriptsize}
        ]
        \node[callout=codecuda] (load-note) {\textbf{Cooperate}\par
        Each thread loads one value from $A$ and one from $B$.};
        \node[callout=blue,below=4mm of load-note] (ready-note) {\textbf{Synchronize}\par
        Wait until both shared tiles are complete.};
        \node[callout=orange,below=4mm of ready-note] (compute-note) {\textbf{Reuse}\par
        One thread accumulates from the cached row and column.};
        \node[callout=codecomment,below=4mm of compute-note] (next-note) {\textbf{Advance}\par
        Protect the cache, move along $K$, and repeat.};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Can we still do better?}

  \begin{block}{Throughput analysis}
    We report a final latency of \(0.36\)s or \(360\)ms for \(n=8192\).
    This is around \(10\times\) the theoretical minimum of $31$ms.
  \end{block}

  After profiling, we see that 76.4\% of warp stalls are still memory-related.
  In other words, we are \textbf{memory bound}.

  \begin{alertblock}{Memory bound means poor hardware utilization.}
    If a kernel is \textbf{memory bound} we can increase throughput by
    moving less data from DRAM. Since we have to read the entirety of both
    input matrices at least once in order to perform the computation, this
    must be done through \textbf{data reuse} via caching in SMEM.
    In general, we can't decrease the total FLOPs required for an operation.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Arithmetic intensity and the Roofline Model}

  \only<1>{
    \begin{block}{Throughput}
      Formally stated, \textbf{throughput} is the total useful work (FLOPs) done
      per unit time. \emph{We previously discussed theoretical peak throughput}.
      \[
        \mathrm{Throughput} = \frac{\mathrm{FLOPs}}{\mathrm{time}}
      \]
    \end{block}

    \begin{example}
      Theoretical peak throughput of our 3090 is $35.6$ TFLOP/s. We observed $360$ms or $0.36$s
      of latency for \(n=8192\), where the total FLOPs required is \(1.10\) TFLOPs.

      Thus, we have an observed throughput of
      \[\frac{1.10\mathrm{ TFLOPs}}{0.36\mathrm{s}} \approx 3.05\mathrm{TFLOP/s}. \]

      Roughly $10$\% of peak throughput.
    \end{example}

  }

  \only<2>{

    \begin{block}{Arithmetic Intensity}
      We define the \textbf{Arithmetic Intensity} (AI) of our kernel as the
      number of FLOPs done per bytes moved from DRAM.
      \[
        \mathrm{AI}=\frac{\mathrm{FLOPs}}{\mathrm{bytes\ moved}}
      \]
    \end{block}

    \begin{example}
      For \(n=8192\) and $32\times32$ tiles, the grid contains
      $256^2=65{,}536$ blocks. Each block takes 256 steps, loading one tile
      from both $A$ and $B$ per step, then writes one tile to $C$.
      \[
        \begin{aligned}
          \mathrm{bytes/block}
          &= \left(256\times2\times32^2+32^2\right)\times4
          = 2{,}101{,}248\ \mathrm{bytes}, \\
          \mathrm{bytes\ total}
          &= 65{,}536\times2{,}101{,}248
          \approx \boxed{137.7\ \mathrm{GB}}.
        \end{aligned}
      \]
    \end{example}
  }

  \only<3>{
    \begin{example}[cont.]
      For \(n=8192\) and $32\times32$ tiles, the grid contains
      $256^2=65{,}536$ blocks. Each block takes 256 steps, loading one tile
      from both $A$ and $B$ per step, then writes one tile to $C$.
      Across the complete grid, the kernel moves
      \[
        65{,}536\left(256\times2\times32^2+32^2\right)\times4
        \approx 137.7\ \mathrm{GB}.
      \]

      With a total work count of $1.10$ TFLOP, its arithmetic intensity is
      \[
        \mathrm{AI}
        = \frac{1.10\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{FLOP}}
        {137.7\times10^9\ \mathrm{bytes}}
        \approx \boxed{8.0\ \mathrm{FLOP/byte}}.
      \]
    \end{example}
  }

  \only<4>{
    \begin{block}{Roofline Model}
      For a given system, we can predict a the upper bound of throughput of a
      kernel given its AI. Up to a certain point, increasing AI will raise the
      upper bound of throughput. However, at the \textbf{ridge point}, we become
      \textbf{compute bound}, and no longer benefit from increasing AI.
    \end{block}
    \begin{example}
      For our 3090, we have $35.6$ TFLOP/s of peak FP32 throughput and $936$ GB/s
      of memory bandwidth. Thus, our ridge point is

      \[ \mathrm{RP} = \frac{35.6 \times 10^{12} \mathrm{FLOP/s}}{936 \times 10^9 \mathrm{bytes/s}} \approx 38.0 \mathrm{FLOPs/byte}\]

    \end{example}
  }

  \only<5>{
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{rtx3090_roofline.png}
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Thread coarsening: Increasing AI}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Basic tiling assigns $1$ element of the output to each thread.
    \item We can \emph{coarsen} each thread, and assign a $TM \times TN$ portion of the output to each thread.
    \item We load larger chunks into SMEM, increasing data reuse and AI.
    \item One loaded $A$ value can update several columns; one loaded $B$ value can update several rows.
  \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

    \item More outputs per thread means fewer memory operations per result.

\section{Conclusion and References}
\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Conclusion}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{AI workloads expose parallel linear algebra.}
      Neural-network layers repeatedly reduce to matrix and tensor operations.
    \item \textbf{GPUs already had the right foundation.}
      Graphics demanded parallel throughput and high memory bandwidth; unified shaders and CUDA made that hardware programmable.
    \item \textbf{Data movement determines real performance.}
      Coalescing, shared-memory tiling, and register reuse move GEMM toward the compute limit.
    \item \textbf{AI is now driving specialization.}
      Tensor Cores, lower precision, and new accelerators trade flexibility for throughput and efficiency.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{alertblock}{Main takeaway}
    Graphics and AI both reward massive parallelism. The GPU succeeded by evolving from a graphics pipeline into a programmable matrix engine.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}
