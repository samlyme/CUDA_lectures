  }
  \only<3>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{nn_2}}
    \[y_2 = x_1 w_{21} + x_2 w_{22} + x_3 w_{23} + x_4 w_{24}\]
  }
  \only<4>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{nn_3}}
    \[y_3 = x_1 w_{31} + x_2 w_{32} + x_3 w_{33} + x_4 w_{34}\]
  }
  \only<5>{\resizebox{0.8\textwidth}{!}{\input{nn_4}}
    \[y_4 = x_1 w_{41} + x_2 w_{42} + x_3 w_{43} + x_4 w_{44}\]
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Linear Algebra Interpretation}
  Here, notice that for each \(y_i\), we are taking a weighted sum of the
  \(x\) elements. That is,

  \[y_i = x_1 w_{i1} + x_2 w_{i2} + x_3 w_{i3} + x_4w_{i4}.\]

  Arranging the \(y_i\) into a vector, we get

  \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
      y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \\ y_4
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
      w_{11} & w_{12} & w_{13} & w_{14} \\
      w_{21} & w_{22} & w_{23} & w_{24} \\
      w_{31} & w_{32} & w_{33} & w_{34} \\
      w_{41} & w_{42} & w_{43} & w_{44}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
      x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \\ x_4
    \end{bmatrix}.
  \]

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Linear Algebra Interpretation}
  We can generalize the number of \(x\)'s and the number of \(y\)'s.

  \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
      y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_m
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
      w_{11} & w_{12} & \cdots & w_{1n} \\
      w_{21} & w_{22} & \cdots & w_{2n} \\
      \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
      w_{m1} & w_{m2} & \cdots & w_{mn}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
      x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n
    \end{bmatrix}.
  \]

  We say, \(n\) is the \emph{input dimension} and \(m\) is the \emph{output dimension}.
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Computing with multiple \(x\) vectors.}
  Suppose we have multiple \(x\) vectors, for which we want to pass through our
  neural network. We can stack the multiple \(x\) vectors in the \textbf{batch},
  which we compute with a matrix multiplication.

  \[
    \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
      \begin{bmatrix}
        y_1^{(1)} & y_1^{(2)} & \cdots & y_1^{(B)} \\
        y_2^{(1)} & y_2^{(2)} & \cdots & y_2^{(B)} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        y_m^{(1)} & y_m^{(2)} & \cdots & y_m^{(B)}
      \end{bmatrix}
      =
      \begin{bmatrix}
        w_{11} & w_{12} & \cdots & w_{1n} \\
        w_{21} & w_{22} & \cdots & w_{2n} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        w_{m1} & w_{m2} & \cdots & w_{mn}
      \end{bmatrix}
      \begin{bmatrix}
        x_1^{(1)} & x_1^{(2)} & \cdots & x_1^{(B)} \\
        x_2^{(1)} & x_2^{(2)} & \cdots & x_2^{(B)} \\
        \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        x_n^{(1)} & x_n^{(2)} & \cdots & x_n^{(B)}
      \end{bmatrix}.
    $}
  \]

  We write this succinctly as \(Y = WX\), where \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times B}\),
  \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times B}\), and \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\).

  \begin{alertblock}{Yippee!}
    We have successfully mapped the batched computation of a \textbf{linear layer}
    a matrix multiplication!
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Composing Layers}

  We can view \(W\) as a function that takes in an \(n\)-dimensional vector and
  maps it to an \(m\)-dimensional vector. \(W \colon \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m\).
  We create deep neural networks by \emph{composing} these transformations, along
  with  \textbf{nonlinearities} \(\sigma_i\).

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[
        scale=0.8, transform shape,
        >=stealth,
        layer/.style={draw, rounded corners, fill=blue!10,
        minimum width=1.6cm, minimum height=1cm, align=center},
        value/.style={align=center, font=\small}
      ]
      \node[value] (x) at (0,0) {input\\\(x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}\)};
      \node[layer] (l1) at (2.5,0) {Layer 1\\\(W_1,\,\sigma_1\)};
      \node[value] (h1) at (4.6,0) {\(h_1\)\\\(\mathbb{R}^{d_1}\)};
      \node[layer] (l2) at (6.6,0) {Layer 2\\\(W_2,\,\sigma_2\)};
      \node[value] (dots) at (8.1,0) {\(\cdots\)};
      \node[layer] (lL) at (9.6,0) {Layer \(L\)\\\(W_L,\,\sigma_L\)};
      \node[value] (y) at (11.7,0) {output\\\(y \in \mathbb{R}^{d_L}\)};

      \draw[->, thick] (x) -- (l1);
      \draw[->, thick] (l1) -- (h1);
      \draw[->, thick] (h1) -- (l2);
      \draw[->, thick] (l2) -- (dots);
      \draw[->, thick] (dots) -- (lL);
      \draw[->, thick] (lL) -- (y);
    \end{tikzpicture}

    \medskip
    \(h_0=x,\qquad h_\ell=\sigma_\ell(W_\ell h_{\ell-1}),\qquad y=h_L\)
  \end{center}

  General neural network architectures like the \textbf{Transformer} and the \textbf{CNN}
  have complex "routings" that can't be represented with just compositions of these
  layers, but all neural networks include this time of layer.

  \begin{block}{Remark}
    Notice that this figure doesn't show the \emph{batch} dimension. However,
    if you work through the dimension mappings, the \emph{batch} dimension \(B\)
    will be unaffected by this composition.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Why GPUs?}
\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{History of GPUs}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 1999: GeForce 256
    \item 2004: Brook Programming, more complex "rendering" pipelines, GP-GPU
    \item 2006: GeForce 8800, Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA)
    \item 2008: Fermi, General Purpose Scientific Compute becomes first class, L1/L2 caches
    \item 2010-2016: incremental improvements to power efficiency, programming models, and multi-GPU systems.
    \item 2017: Volta, AI-specialization: tensor cores designed around deep learning primitives
    \item 2022: Hopper, Transformer Engine, further specializing into AI
    \item 2026: Since 2022, we have only really seen incremental improvements.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Parallelism}
  Graphics rendering is a naturally parallel task. Each pixel does the same work.

  CPUs can't keep up, so we trade logical capability for \textbf{throughput}.
  Each \emph{thread} on the GPU can only do simple things, but we have many of
  them. GPU's can't do some of the more algorithmically complex tasks, but can
  do a a large amount of simple tasks much faster.

  That is, a CPU has few threads that are very capable. A GPU has many dumb threads.
\end{frame}
